## Abstract

Legged robots that share corridors and offices with people are often much slower than the people around them, and
pedestrians frequently change direction shortly before a potential contact. We study velocity-level local planning for a
Unitree Go2 quadruped under these conditions and propose C1, a sampling-based model predictive controller (MPPI) that combines
(i) a time-discounted, maximum-over-horizon geometric risk between a two-disc body model and constant-velocity pedestrian
predictions, (ii) maximum-velocity evasive primitives injected as additional samples, (iii) a temperature rule that avoids
degenerate weighting when most samples collide, together with (iv) a LiDAR person tracker and (v) an online velocity-response
model that are shared with all tuned baselines. No single component is new; the contribution is the combination for the slow-robot
regime and a rigorous evaluation. The design was frozen with cryptographic hashes and the analysis was pre-registered before a
one-shot test on 1000 unseen generated worlds × 5 dynamics/crowd conditions (35 000 episodes). C1 reached the goal without any
collision in 85.3 % of test episodes, versus 78.3 % for a tuned MPPI with identical perception (+7.0 points, 95 % CI [5.7, 8.3]),
80.3 % for a re-implementation of PGIF-MPPI (+5.0 [3.8, 6.2]) and 82.6 % for a re-implementation of DRA-MPPI (+2.7 [1.5, 3.8]);
all Holm-adjusted p < 1e-4, and the gaps replicated on four further disjoint seed sets. The advantage is condition-dependent: it
is largest for slow CHAMP-like dynamics and vanishes for a fast robot facing Helbing-type pedestrians, and in static-only worlds C1
is non-inferior to, but 0.95 points below, tuned MPPI. In a separate pre-registered Gazebo test with the CHAMP gait (680 worlds),
C1 succeeded in 80.7 % versus 65.7 % for tuned MPPI (exact McNemar p = 1.5e-13). With an independent Isaac Lab body (600 worlds), C1 reached
88.5 %, versus 79.5 % for tuned MPPI and 78.3 % for PGIF-MPPI (Holm p ≤ 2.2e-6), but it was **not** better than DRA-MPPI (87.2 %, p = 0.46).

## 1. Introduction

Service and assistant robots increasingly operate in spaces designed for people. A quadruped such as the Unitree Go2 can walk fast
on paper, but when it runs a conservative gait controller, carries sensors, or is commanded through a simulated gait (e.g. CHAMP in
Gazebo, which clips forward commands at 0.30 m/s; Table T8), it moves about three times slower than a walking adult
(≈0.9 m/s). For such a robot, the dominant failure in our development experiments was not a static obstacle but a pedestrian who
turned or restarted walking close to the robot: standard MPPI [@williams2018itmpc] with a costmap-style people term reacts too late,
because the cost of a "late" pedestrian becomes visible only when every sample is already in collision. Similar failure reports appear
in learning-based crowd navigation (sudden direction changes in HEIGHT [@liu2026height] and DRL-VO [@xie2023drlvo]; dynamic objects
faster than the recovery policy in ABS [@he2024abs]) and on a real Go2 with Nav2-MPPI [@wang2026video].

Worst-case approaches (reachability, pursuit–evasion [@isaacs1965differential]) are attractive but presuppose a robot that is at least
nearly as fast as the pedestrian; recent HJ-based safety filters for crowds use robot speeds comparable to pedestrians
[@derajic2026cncbf]. With a speed ratio of ≈0.3 we instead pursue *finite-horizon, risk-weighted* planning without guarantees and
ask a narrow empirical question: **does a risk-aware MPPI tailored to this regime beat well-tuned MPPI-family baselines that share
the same perception, under a protocol that prevents us from fooling ourselves?**

**Contributions.**
1. **C1**, an MPPI variant for slow legged robots among late-turning pedestrians that combines a discounted max-over-time geometric
   risk on a two-disc body, evasive maximum-velocity primitive samples (an application of biased sampling [@trevisan2024biased]),
   a quantile-based temperature rule with graded collision penalties, a person tracker with stop-retention and centroid compensation,
   and an online velocity-response model. We state explicitly that each ingredient has precedents (Section 2).
2. **A pre-registered, hash-frozen evaluation** with disjoint development, confirmation, validation and test seed sets; tuned
   baselines that share tracker, response model, sample budget and static costs; three dynamics regimes (two identifications of CHAMP
   and a literature/measurement-based Go2 range) × two pedestrian models; a static-only arm; two further pre-registered test
   families with different bodies (Gazebo/CHAMP, 680 worlds; Isaac Lab learned policy, 600 worlds); and Holm-corrected paired statistics. All tables and figures regenerate from raw logs with one deterministic script.
3. **An honest account of where the method does not help**: the advantage over DRA-MPPI is small and significant in only one of five
   conditions; with a fast robot and Helbing-type pedestrians C1 ties all baselines; static-only performance is slightly worse; the tuning
   budget favoured C1; and Gazebo exhibits tracker artefacts from body wobble.

## 2. Related Work

**MPPI and sampling-based MPC.** MPPI [@williams2018itmpc] updates a control sequence by exponentially weighting sampled rollouts.
Log-MPPI changes the sampling distribution for cluttered spaces [@mohamed2022logmppi]; Biased-MPPI injects samples from ancillary
controllers (e.g. braking, go-slow) and shows that the importance weights then reduce to exp(−S/λ) at the price of a bias, together
with an adaptive temperature [@trevisan2024biased]. Nav2 provides a production MPPI in which people enter only as instantaneous
costmap cells [@macenski2023desks]. Contingency-MPPI requires every nominal state to keep a feasible path to a given safe set,
using nested MPPI [@jung2025contingency]; SCRAMPPI replaces the inner search by an HJ value lookup [@srirangam2026scrampi], and
DualGuard-MPPI combines HJ filtering with MPPI [@borquez2025dualguard]; these works address static or revealed environments rather
than pedestrians. *Relation to C1:* our evasive primitives are an instance of biased sampling, and our temperature rule is a
heuristic related to adaptive-λ schemes; neither is claimed as new.

**MPPI/MPC among pedestrians.** PGIF-MPPI adds an anisotropic Gaussian field around constant-velocity pedestrian predictions
(σ∥ = 1.2 + 0.5 s ahead, 0.5 m behind, σ⊥ = 0.6 m), summed over the horizon without discounting, with ground-truth pedestrian states
and a 2 m/s unicycle robot [@mundane2026pgif]. DRA-MPPI estimates the joint collision probability of each rollout by Monte Carlo
(20 000 points, K = 400) under Gaussian or multi-modal (Markov turning) predictions and penalises it softly and with a hard threshold
[@trevisan2025dramppi]; it includes the possibility that pedestrians turn, but open-loop, with a fast robot (2 m/s reference) in a 6 m
corridor. C2U-MPPI turns a Gaussian chance constraint with full covariance into a deterministic Mahalanobis threshold and propagates
robot uncertainty with the unscented transform; pedestrians are predicted with a constant-velocity Kalman filter and evaluated
mostly in a non-cooperative mode with robot speeds of 1–2 m/s [@mohamed2025c2u]. Risk-aware MPPI for stochastic hybrid systems
models attention-dependent pedestrian modes [@parwana2024riskmppi], and MPPIPS-HWP uses learned trajectory prediction plus a warning
policy [@long2025mppips]. CoCoNav combines conformal prediction with MPC and backup checks on a legged robot [@guo2026coconav].
*Relation to C1:* the closest baselines are PGIF-MPPI and DRA-MPPI, which we re-implement with the same perception (Section 5.3).
C1 differs in using a discounted *maximum* over time of a clearance-based risk on a two-disc body, evasive primitives, and a temperature
correction, in a regime where the robot is ≈3× slower than pedestrians.

**Safety filters, reachability and pursuit–evasion.** CN-CBF learns an HJ reach-avoid value on the relative state of a robot and a
unicycle pedestrian with adversarial turn rate |ω| ≤ 0.35 rad/s, combines pedestrians with a softmin and filters an MPC command with a
CBF-QP; it reports up to 18 % higher success than the strongest baseline with non-reactive HuNavSim pedestrians and a robot speed of
0.25–1.0 m/s [@derajic2026cncbf; @derajic2026thesis]. Policy-library CBFs check a set of backup policies by parallel rollouts
[@kim2026plcbf]. Stochastic reachable sets of hybrid moving obstacles, precomputed and turned into potential fields, scale to hundreds
of obstacles [@malone2017hybrid]. Pursuit–evasion theory (the homicidal-chauffeur game [@isaacs1965differential; @pachter2019classical])
characterises when a faster, turn-limited pursuer can always capture a slower agile evader. With speed ratio γ = v_e/v_p < 1, capture
radius l and pursuer turning radius R = v_p/ω, Isaacs' classical solution (as restated in [@bopardikar2007cooperative, Eq. (2)]) admits
an evasion policy when l/R ≤ √(1−γ²) + γ·arcsin γ − 1. Mapping a pedestrian to the pursuer and the robot to the evader (v_e = 0.3 m/s,
v_p = 0.9–1.3 m/s, a gentle ω = 0.35 rad/s as in [@derajic2026cncbf], l = 0.43–0.64 m for our two body discs), l/R exceeds this bound
by a factor of 3.0–6.4, so no worst-case escape guarantee of this kind is available to a slow legged robot (point-robot idealisation;
walls and the robot's own turn-rate limit only make it worse). *Relation to C1:* C1 offers no guarantee;
it is a finite-horizon, risk-weighted planner for the regime in which worst-case evasion is generally not available.

**Body shape and legged robots.** Velocity-obstacle constraints for convex (capsule-shaped) non-holonomic robots have been used for
reactive crowd navigation on a wheelchair [@gonon2021reactive], and anisotropic velocity limits of quadrupeds have been exploited in
planning [@zhang2024anisotropy]. Velocity-command dynamics of Unitree quadrupeds have been identified with motion capture
[@yun2025safe; @mei2024pwc]. C1's two-disc body is therefore not new.

**Learning-based crowd navigation and evaluation.** Reinforcement-learning planners [@chen2019crowd; @xie2023drlvo; @liu2026height]
and the freezing-robot problem [@trautman2010unfreezing] frame the field; constant-velocity prediction remains a strong baseline
[@scholler2020cvm; @poddar2023crowd]. Evaluation guidelines call for multiple metrics, edge cases and explicit baseline justification
[@francis2025principles]. Our protocol follows these recommendations and adds pre-registration and frozen code.

## 3. Problem Setup

The robot receives a goal in an indoor map and must reach it (navigation tolerance 0.2 m; success requires a final position error below 0.35 m) within 300 s while avoiding
static obstacles and 2–6 pedestrians. The local planner outputs body-frame velocity commands u = (v_x, v_y, ω_z) at 10 Hz, executed by the
robot's own locomotion controller (CHAMP [@champ] in Gazebo, a learned policy in Isaac Lab, or Unitree's SportClient on the real robot). Sensing is a
2-D LiDAR scan (range used for tracking 5.5 m, blind zone ≈0.75 m around the body) plus a forward depth camera (±43°, 0.3–4 m); pedestrians
are not given to the planner and must be tracked. The robot footprint is a 0.64 × 0.50 m rectangle for collision checking, modelled inside
the planner by two discs. Pedestrians (radius 0.22 m, desired speed N(0.9, 0.15²) m/s clipped to [0.6, 1.2]) walk between random goals on
A\* paths with social forces; 30 % are *distracted* and ignore the robot. **Success** means reaching the goal with no contact of any kind
(person or static); the episode ends at the first contact. This any-contact criterion is the pre-registered primary metric; secondary
metrics are collision types, stuck episodes, timeouts and time to goal.

## 4. Method

Figure F0 (`figures/F0_architecture.pdf`) shows the architecture. Components marked "shared" are used by C1 *and* by every tuned
baseline, so that comparisons isolate the planner.

### 4.1 Person tracker v3 (shared)
LiDAR points are clustered on a local grid after removing points that fall in cells already known to be static (background
subtraction), so that people walking next to furniture are not merged with it. Motion evidence is accumulated per point (fraction of
points falling into previously free cells) rather than from the cluster centroid. Each track is filtered by a constant-velocity Kalman
filter [@kalman1960]. Because a LiDAR sees only the near side of a person, the centroid is shifted away from the robot by 0.64 × the
cluster radius. Tracks are confirmed after 2 scans; a person who stops remains a person for up to 6 s (the previous tracker converted
stopped people into static obstacles, which caused ≈6 of 16 analysed collisions); tracks are held through short occlusions and the
blind zone; and time spent waiting for a tracked person is not counted as being stuck. On 10 development worlds the mean position /
velocity errors were 0.088 m / 0.137 m/s, versus 0.184 m / 0.194 m/s for the previous tracker.

### 4.2 Online velocity-response model, RespModel (shared)
For each axis (forward, reverse, lateral, yaw) the measured velocity is modelled as v = s·tanh(g·u/s) followed by a first-order lag,
where g is a gain and s a saturation. A grid posterior over (g, s) is updated online from (command, measured velocity) pairs; the
simulator's parameters are never read. All rollouts of all tuned planners propagate commands through this model.

### 4.3 Risk-aware MPPI (C1)
*Sampling.* At each step, K = 600 control sequences over T = 24 steps of 0.1 s are drawn around the previous solution with per-axis
Gaussian noise (σ = 0.22, 0.14, 0.55 for v_x, v_y, ω_z) and rolled out through RespModel. In addition, 96 **evasive primitives** are
evaluated: constant maximum-velocity commands in 16 directions × 3 yaw modes (ω_z ∈ {0, ±0.7} rad/s), applied either for the full
horizon or for the first 1.2 s. Their purpose is practical: with the MPPI noise level, a full-speed sidestep for 1.5 s is essentially never
sampled. As in Biased-MPPI [@trevisan2024biased], these samples simply enter the weighted average.

*Cost.* S_k = S_path + S_static + S_hit + S_geo. S_path and S_static are the path-following and static-obstacle terms of the baseline
MPPI (shared). The graded collision term S_hit = c_hit·(1 − τ_first/T) + 0.5·c_hit·max_t pen(t)/0.3 with c_hit = 300 rewards
rollouts that collide later and less deeply, instead of a flat penalty that makes all colliding samples equal.

*Geometric risk.* For the three nearest tracked pedestrians within 4.5 m, a constant-velocity prediction q_j(t) is formed. The robot body
along rollout k is represented by two discs at ±c along the heading, and d_jk(t) is the clearance between these discs and the
pedestrian disc (radius sum plus 0.03 m). The risk is

  S_geo,k = w_geo · Σ_j max_t  e^(−t/τ_g) · φ(d_jk(t)),  φ(d) = e^(−d/ℓ) if d > 0, and 1 − d otherwise,

with τ_g = 1.2 s, ℓ = 0.12 m, w_geo = 40, evaluated every third step. The *maximum over time* makes a rollout as risky as its worst
predicted encounter rather than accumulating risk along the horizon, and the *discount* reflects that a distant encounter can still be
re-planned when more is observed (recourse) — the main difference from the summed, undiscounted field of PGIF-MPPI [@mundane2026pgif].
A variant with a geodesic intention fan instead of constant velocity was evaluated during development and did not help.

*Temperature.* Standard MPPI implementations set λ from a central statistic of the costs; when more than half the samples collide,
this makes all weights nearly equal and the robot averages contradictory manoeuvres. C1 sets λ = 0.3·(Q₀.₁₅(S) − min S), where Q₀.₁₅
is the 15 % quantile, so that weight concentrates on the best samples. The command is u = Σ_k w_k u_k with w_k ∝ exp(−(S_k − min S)/λ).

### 4.4 Optional command shaper `~cs` (deployment option, not part of C1)
For legged controllers that are fragile at slow sideways/backward walking with turning, an optional shaper limits |ω_z| ≤ 0.6 rad/s when
|v_y| > 0.15 m/s or v_x < −0.05 m/s, and rate-limits |Δv_y| ≤ 1.5 m/s² and |Δω_z| ≤ 2.5 rad/s²; stop commands pass unchanged. It is
not used in any comparison below; its effect is reported in Section 6.9.

### 4.5 Baselines (all with tracker v3, RespModel, K = 600, T = 24 and the same static costs)
- **B1** — MPPI with a tuned people cost (people weight 100, length 0.5 m).
- **PGIF\*** — our re-implementation of PGIF-MPPI [@mundane2026pgif] from its equations, with a two-disc body, weights rescaled to our
  cost scale and tuned (w_h = 300, σ scale 0.7, optional hard penalty 5000). The original uses ground-truth pedestrians; ours uses the tracker.
- **DRA\*** — our re-implementation of DRA-MPPI [@trevisan2025dramppi]: per-step collision probability of each disc with a Gaussian
  pedestrian (σ_t = σ_w·√(dt·t)), computed exactly with a non-central χ² lookup table instead of Monte Carlo, combined as
  1 − ∏(1 − P); soft weight 300, hard weight 0 (best on DEV), σ_w = 1.2, σ₀ = 0.05. The multi-modal turning model was not included.
- **B0** — MPPI with the original tracker (no RespModel), people weight 32; **DWA** and **log-MPPI** [@mohamed2022logmppi], tuned,
  with tracker v3 and RespModel. B0, DWA and log-MPPI are reported descriptively.

## 5. Experimental Protocol

### 5.1 Dynamics identification (Table T8)
*CHAMP.* From Gazebo logs (results/sysid.npz, 150 segments) we found that CHAMP's apparent forward saturation at 0.29 m/s is the
command clipping of its gait configuration (0.30/0.25/0.50 for v_x/v_y/ω_z) plus a backward drift of ≈0.053 m/s while stepping. Two regimes
are used: `champ` (tanh saturation 0.26–0.32 m/s, reverse ×1.10–1.35) and `champ2` (clipping + drift). The 2-s open-loop prediction error
on the CHAMP log is 0.169 m for v = u, 0.088 m for `champ` and 0.062 m for `champ2`.
*Go2.* Our own logs of the real robot contain no velocity commands, so gain, delay and time constants could not be identified. The `go2`
regime samples per episode from ranges combining our measurements (odometry scale, stopping distance ≈0.28 s × v₀ as an upper bound,
body wobble, acceleration lower bounds) with literature values (gain 0.95 [0.75–1.20], τ_x 0.28 s [0.15–0.45]) and assumptions (delay 0.10 s
[0.04–0.20]) [@yun2025safe; @mei2024pwc].
*Isaac Lab.* The official Isaac Lab [@nvidia2025isaaclab; @mittal2023orbit] Go2 rough-terrain policy tracks forward commands up to
1 m/s with gain 0.99 [0.97, 1.00] at 0.4–0.6 m/s, confirming that the 0.3 m/s limit belongs to CHAMP, not to the Go2 body.

### 5.2 Simulation tiers
*fastsim* is a 2-D simulator with a virtual clock that executes the unmodified planner, tracker and RespModel code with simulated rays,
depth camera, body dynamics (one regime per cell) and pedestrians; it is deterministic per (world, episode) and the noise streams are
independent of the planner, so all planners face the same world and pedestrians (paired design). *Gazebo* (Harmonic, CHAMP gait, 16-beam
LiDAR, pedestrians moved by a separate process) is a separate pre-registered family. *Isaac Lab* replaces only the body by the learned
policy (rays, crowd and tracker remain fastsim code), run with several robots in lock-step (4 in the test, 8 in the pilot).

### 5.3 Worlds and pedestrian models
Worlds are generated per seed from five families (office, warehouse, lobby, corridor with doors, clutter; 12–18 m × 9–14 m). The test
set contains 194–208 worlds per family and 2–6 pedestrians (191–207 worlds per count). Two pedestrian models are used: `base`
(social forces with wall repulsion, contact forces and noise) and `helb`, a Helbing–Molnár-type circular repulsive potential
[@helbing1995social] with amplitude V₀/σ = 2.1/0.3 and an anisotropic weight; walls, goals, contacts and noise are unchanged. A *cell* is a
(dynamics, pedestrian) pair; five cells are used: CHAMP/base, CHAMP2/base, Go2/base, CHAMP2/Helbing and Go2/Helbing.

### 5.4 Seed splits, freezing and pre-registration
Development used seeds 0–999. The candidate was locked before each of three confirmation rounds (R1: 300–799, R2: 1000–1499, R3:
1500–1999; each run once). After R3, the design was **frozen**: SHA-256 hashes of all planner, simulator and analysis files and a code
snapshot were recorded. Validation (VAL, seeds 2000–2399) and the test were pre-registered (comparisons, metric, tests, sample size,
exclusion rules, stopping rule) before any VAL/TEST result was seen. The fastsim TEST uses the first 1000 worlds with people at or above seed
100000 (seeds 100000–101386), one episode per world, cell and planner, executed once from a read-only snapshot whose hashes were checked
before every cell. Power analysis on R2+R3 indicated ≈93 % power for a true C1 − DRA\* difference of +2 points at n = 1000. Four documented
deviations exist: (#1) the Gazebo smoke-test criterion S4 failed (Section 8) and Gazebo TEST was run as registered; (#2) a static-only
arm was added before running it; (#3) the Isaac arm ran in parallel with Gazebo TEST with 4 instead of 8 robots, CPU-pinned at low priority and
paused automatically if the Gazebo real-time factor dropped below 0.9 (it never did); (#4) because the hashed Isaac client accepts only
development/validation seeds, a 15-line wrapper with a fixed list of test worlds was used, and the analysis script was written and hashed
before any result was seen.

### 5.5 Statistics
For each world w and baseline X, d_w is the mean over the five cells of success(C1) − success(X). The primary family {C1 vs B1, PGIF\*,
DRA\*} is tested with a two-sided sign-flip permutation test over worlds (200 000 flips) and a percentile bootstrap CI (10 000 resamples of
worlds), with Holm correction [@holm1979]; "C1 better than X" requires p_Holm < 0.05 and d̄ > 0. Secondary analyses: per-cell exact
McNemar tests [@mcnemar1947] with Holm over 15, Wilson intervals [@wilson1927] for rates, failure types, and descriptive differences
to B0, DWA and log-MPPI. Infrastructure exceptions in every planner of a world would remove the world; none occurred. All tables are
regenerated by one script with fixed seeds (`make_all.py`), which reproduces the pre-registered analysis output byte for byte.

+7.9, +12.3, +6.1 and +6.7 points in CHAMP/base, CHAMP2/base, Go2/base and CHAMP2/Helbing (Holm ≤ 2.6e-5) and +1.8 in Go2/Helbing
(Holm 0.48). Against PGIF\*, the three base-crowd cells were significant (+6.7, +8.0, +6.1; Holm ≤ 8.4e-5) and both Helbing cells were
not (+2.7, Holm 0.28; +1.4, Holm 0.86). Against DRA\*, only CHAMP2/base was significant (+5.9, 122 vs 63 discordant pairs, Holm 1.4e-4);
CHAMP/base +1.7, Go2/base +1.4, CHAMP2/Helbing +3.5 (Holm 0.064) and Go2/Helbing +0.8 (Holm 0.86) were not.

### 6.4 Failure modes (Table T4, Fig. F4)
Almost all failures were person collisions: 13.8 % of C1 episodes versus 16.7 % (DRA\*), 19.0 % (PGIF\*), 20.5 % (B1) and 30.8 % (B0).
Static collisions, stuck episodes, timeouts and no-path outcomes were each below 1 % for every planner (C1: 0.12, 0.44, 0.32, 0.02 %).
Mean time to goal over successful episodes was 59.0 s for C1, 58.5 s for DRA\*, 56.0 s for PGIF\* and 70.8 s for B1 (conditional on
success, hence descriptive).

### 6.5 Static-only worlds (Table T5)
On all 387 test-range worlds without people (3 regimes), success was 96–99.5 % for every planner and every failure but one B0 contact was
"stuck". C1 achieved 96.9 % versus 97.8 % for B1, Δ = −0.95 [−1.55, −0.34]: the pre-registered non-inferiority margin (−2 points) is met,
but C1 is measurably worse, with more stuck episodes (3.1 % vs 2.2 %). DWA (99.5 %) and B0 (99.0 %) were best.

### 6.6 Ablation (Table T6, Fig. F5)
In round R2 (348 worlds × 3 base cells), removing tracker v3 cost 5.8 points [−8.7, −3.0] (Holm 0.0014) and removing the evasive
primitives 4.2 [−6.7, −1.7] (Holm 0.029); replacing the geometric risk by the plain people cost (−2.2), removing the temperature rule (−1.1),
the discount (−0.7) or RespModel (−0.4) were not significant individually. In R3, simpler variants were tested under a pre-registered
non-inferiority rule (margin −2): MPPI + primitives without/with RespModel (−5.66 [−7.79, −3.59] / −5.66 [−7.62, −3.70]) and C1 without
temperature rule, discount and RespModel (−0.84 [−2.69, +0.95]); none qualified, so the full C1 was frozen. The geometric risk and the
temperature rule appear partly interchangeable (removing both costs ≈5.7 points, either alone ≤ 2.2), which we did not test directly.

### 6.7 Replication (Table T10, Fig. F3)
Recomputed with the test procedure, C1 − B1 was +5.73 (R1), +6.61 (R2), +7.17 (R3), +9.08 (VAL) and +6.96 (TEST); C1 − PGIF\* +5.23, +6.05,
+6.22 and +4.98 (R2, R3, VAL, TEST); C1 − DRA\* +3.16, +2.80, +4.51 and +2.66, with every 95 % CI excluding zero. Only TEST is
confirmatory; the sets are not pooled.

### 6.8 Computation (Table T7)
C1 required 8.6 ms per step on average (p99 10.9 ms) in numpy on a laptop CPU, comparable to B1 (8.7 / 12.1 ms), PGIF\* (9.1 / 11.1 ms) and
DRA\* (9.2 / 12.7 ms, plus a one-off 205 ms table build). In the Gazebo smoke test the measured C1 step was 7.5 ms median, 15.4 ms p95,
48 ms max over 16 343 steps, with a command period of 0.101 s median.

### 6.9 Fidelity and independent-body cross-check (Table T9)
On 8 development office tasks and 12 planners, fastsim rankings agreed with 960 Gazebo episodes with Spearman ρ = 0.90 (`champ`) and
0.59 (`champ2`); the difference (+0.31, task-bootstrap CI [−0.13, +0.42]) is inconclusive. Replacing the body by the Isaac Lab policy on
100 development worlds preserved the ranking of five planners (Kendall τ 1.00 vs fastsim go2 and champ, 0.80 vs champ2); C1 succeeded in
83/100 vs 75/100 for B1 (p = 0.22, pilot). A later deterministic re-run gave 87 vs 73 (McNemar +20/−6, p = 0.009; exploratory). Isaac
policy falls (6 for C1 and 5 for B1 in the pilot) were linked to slow reverse/turning commands; the optional shaper `~cs` removed falls
(0/200) but cost 5–8 points in fastsim go2 (not significant, n = 100).

### 6.10 Gazebo TEST (Table T11, Fig. F6)
*Design (pre-registered, separate family).* The first 680 worlds of the fastsim test set were converted to Gazebo worlds (4 worlds per file).
The robot used the CHAMP gait (commands clipped at 0.30 m/s), and pedestrians were moved by a separate process with the base model. Each
world was run once per planner, both planners on the same simulator instance in random order. The primary test was C1 vs B1 with an exact
two-sided McNemar test at α = 0.05; DRA\* was gated on this test and is exploratory. All 680 worlds were completed (0 dropped), the code
hashes matched before every group (1360/1360 episodes), and the per-episode real-time factor had median 0.985 (min 0.841).

*Result.* C1 succeeded in 549/680 worlds = **80.7 %** [Wilson 77.6, 83.5], B1 in 447/680 = **65.7 %** [62.1, 69.2]: **+15.0 points**, with
149 worlds where only C1 succeeded and 47 where only B1 did; exact McNemar **p = 1.5e-13**. The pre-registered primary test therefore passes.

*Failures.* C1 had 123 collision episodes (122 person, 1 static) against 211 for B1 (199 person, 12 static). The noisy at-fault flag was set in
54 and 89 of them, respectively. Mean time to goal over successes was 64.1 s for C1 and 74.8 s for B1.

*Comparison with fastsim on the same worlds.* fastsim predicted a smaller gap: C1 − B1 = +8.4 points in CHAMP/base and +12.2 in CHAMP2/base,
versus +15.0 in Gazebo. Per-world outcomes agreed with fastsim CHAMP/base in 76.5 % of worlds for C1 and 65.4 % for B1.

*Caveats.* All Gazebo results must be read with the S4 smoke-test failure (Section 8): the shared tracker sees ghost "people" on static
objects because the simulated CHAMP body wobbles. This affects both planners, but through different planning layers. A post-hoc,
exploratory analysis of the logged tracks (research/coord_gz_ghost_analysis.py; a track > 1 m from every true pedestrian and within 3 m
of the robot counts as a ghost) shows that ghosts do not explain the larger Gazebo gap: (i) both planners saw the same ghost exposure
(7.4 % vs 7.7 % of 0.1 s steps); (ii) splitting worlds into terciles of ghost exposure, the C1−B1 gap stays at +14.5, +16.7 and +13.7
points (also for ghost radii of 0.7 m and 1.5 m), even though success falls with exposure for both planners; (iii) against fastsim with
the re-identified CHAMP model (champ2) the Gazebo gap is only +2.8 points larger [−2.6, 8.2] (paired bootstrap), whereas against the
original CHAMP model it is +6.6 [1.5, 11.8]. The extra gap therefore mostly reflects dynamics mismatch of the original fastsim CHAMP
model; the remainder comes from B1 static contacts (12 vs 1) and no-contact failures (22 vs 8) in Gazebo. Ghost exposure also grows
with crowd density, so (ii) is associational, not causal. As a further post-hoc robustness check of the crowd model, we implemented the Helbing–Molnár (1995) elliptical potential exactly as written (V0 = 2.1 m²/s², σ = 0.3 m, Δt = 2 s, 200° sight cone with c = 0.5, U0 = 10 m²/s², R = 0.2 m, no noise) and re-ran the 1000 fastsim TEST worlds: C1 still beat B1 (+3.5 / +7.2 points for go2 / CHAMP2) and PGIF* (+7.1 / +6.2), tied DRA* under go2 (−0.5, p = 0.76) and beat it under CHAMP2 (+4.7). Our implementation, however, does not reproduce lane formation in counter-flow (also when using the desired direction and speed in Eq. (4)); a circular potential does. The conclusions therefore hold for a lane-forming crowd (our "helb" cell) and for a non-lane-forming one, but the faithful-HM95 crowd is verified only at the level of equations, not behaviour. A post-hoc integrity check also found a start-up bug in the shared harness:
in 75 of 1360 episodes (the first episode of an evaluation run) stale tracks from the default controller made the tracker raise for the
first ≈4 s, so the map and tracks were not updated. Affected episodes were not worse (C1 91 %, B1 63 %), and excluding every affected
world leaves the primary result unchanged (N = 605: 79.7 % vs 65.1 %, exact McNemar p = 8.5e-12).

*DRA\* in Gazebo* (gated, exploratory, first 340 worlds; the gate opened because C1 vs B1 passed). C1 succeeded in 282/340 = 82.9 %
[78.6, 86.6], DRA\* in 238/340 = 70.0 % [64.9, 74.6] (discordant 66/22, exact McNemar p = 2.9e-6). On the same 340 worlds fastsim
gave only +2.1 (CHAMP) and +5.0 (CHAMP2) points, so DRA\* lost about 10 points moving to Gazebo while C1 did not. Checks: excluding
start-up-bug worlds and four DRA\* episodes with mean RTF < 0.8 for 29–50 s (infrastructure faults under Section 5 that the runner did
not retry) leaves 81.4 % vs 70.3 % (N = 296, p = 1.8e-4). Because DRA\* ran about 5 h after C1/B1, we re-ran C1 on the first 80 of these
worlds afterwards: 60/80 versus 58/80 originally (discordant 16/14, p = 0.86), so run conditions did not drift for C1. DRA\* episodes ran
at a slightly lower RTF (median 0.933 vs 0.967) because other CPU work ran concurrently; DRA\* success rose from 68 % to 75 % from the
lowest to the highest RTF tercile, so load may explain a few points but not the gap. This comparison is exploratory and is not part of
the confirmatory family.

Fig. F6 shows the first world, in seed order, where C1 succeeded and B1 had a person contact (selected by a fixed rule; illustrative only).

### 6.11 Isaac Lab TEST (Table T11b)
*Design (pre-registered, separate exploratory family).* The body was the official Isaac Lab Go2 rough-terrain policy with observation noise.
We used the first 600 test worlds with the base crowd and 4 robots in lock-step throughout (deviations #3–#4). Falls count as failures.
The primary family was C1 vs B1, PGIF\* and DRA\*, each with an exact McNemar test and Holm correction over 3. There were 0 exclusions.

*Result.* Success was **88.5 %** for C1 [85.7, 90.8], 87.2 % for DRA\*, 79.5 % for B1 and 78.3 % for PGIF\*.
- C1 − B1 = +9.0 points (88 vs 34 discordant worlds, Holm p = 2.2e-6).
- C1 − PGIF\* = +10.2 points (90 vs 29, Holm p = 5.6e-8).
- **C1 − DRA\* = +1.3 points (48 vs 40, p = 0.46): not significant.**

*Falls.* Fall rates were 1.3 % (C1), 5.0 % (B1), 3.2 % (PGIF\*) and 2.3 % (DRA\*), so part of C1's Isaac advantage is that it falls less.
Excluding every pair in which either robot fell (descriptive), the discordant counts were 64/28 against B1, 77/23 against PGIF\* and 37/33
against DRA\*. The falls are a property of the learned Isaac policy (Section 6.9), not of the Unitree controller.

*Agreement with fastsim on the same 600 worlds.* The ranking C1 > DRA\* > {B1, PGIF\*} held in every fastsim cell. Kendall τ was 1.00 for
Go2/base and 0.67 for the other four cells; τ < 1 only because B1 and PGIF\* swap places, 1–3 points apart. The Isaac C1 − B1 gap (+9.0) lies
inside the fastsim range (+1.2 in Go2/Helbing to +12.0 in CHAMP2/base).

*Caveat.* **Isaac is independent of fastsim only in the body dynamics**: rays, pedestrians and the tracker are the same code.

## 7. Discussion

*Where the gain comes from.* Two components carry most of the measurable effect: the tracker (shared with all tuned baselines, so it
does not contribute to the primary differences) and the evasive primitives. The pattern across cells is consistent with the motivation:
the advantage is largest when the robot is slow relative to pedestrians (CHAMP regimes, commands clipped at 0.3 m/s) and pedestrians
turn late (base model, 30 % distracted), and it disappears when the robot is fast (Go2 regime) and pedestrians avoid it more strongly
(Helbing model), where all risk-aware planners saturate near 90 %.

*Three tiers, one picture.* The C1 − B1 gap was positive in fastsim (+7.0 pooled), Gazebo (+15.0) and Isaac (+9.0); C1 − PGIF\* likewise
(+5.0 fastsim, +10.2 Isaac). The tiers share the planner code, the perception code (fastsim, Isaac) and the generated worlds, so they are not
independent replications of the whole system. What they vary is the body: kinematic regimes, the CHAMP gait with physics and a 16-beam
LiDAR, and a learned locomotion policy. The direction of the main effect survived each change.

*Relation to DRA-MPPI.* DRA\* was the strongest baseline in every tier where it was run. Its probabilistic cost already accounts for
prediction uncertainty. The remaining C1 advantage is +2.7 points pooled in fastsim, significant in only one of five conditions, and
**not significant on the independent Isaac body** (+1.3, p = 0.46). We therefore do not claim to outperform DRA-MPPI. A fair statement:
at equal perception and sample budget, C1 was never worse than DRA\* in any condition or tier, and was better on average in fastsim.

*Static performance.* The person layer costs about one point of success in static-only worlds, mostly through waiting/stuck behaviour. The
same tracker mechanism produced ghost tracks in Gazebo. A tilt-robust static filter for the tracker is the most direct next step.

*What the protocol buys.* Freezing, pre-registration and disjoint seed sets removed several effects that looked promising during
development (e.g. a turning-hazard cost and occlusion-aware phantoms were rejected by pre-registered rules), and the reported effects
replicated across five seed sets. The same protocol exposed an unequal tuning budget, which we disclose below rather than correct post hoc.

## 8. Limitations
(Condensed from `DRAFT_limitations.md`; keep all items in the final paper.)
1. **Simulation only.** Confirmatory evidence comes from three simulators: fastsim (primary), Gazebo/CHAMP and Isaac Lab. No real-robot
   navigation run with C1 exists. Isaac is independent of fastsim only in the body dynamics, and Gazebo shares the worlds and planner code.
   The Gazebo DRA\* arm is pending and exploratory.
2. **Small, condition-dependent advantage over DRA-MPPI\***: +2.66 [1.52, 3.80] pooled in fastsim, significant only in CHAMP2/base and
   null in Go2/Helbing, where C1 also ties B1 and PGIF\*. **Not significant in Isaac** (+1.3, p = 0.46).
3. **Tuning budget favoured C1**: ≈60 configurations on all five cells for C1's family versus 35 each for PGIF\*/DRA\*, tuned only on
   CHAMP/base seeds 0–149 (and 21 for B1's family). This violates our own rule that baselines receive at least the same budget.
4. **Static-only worlds**: C1 −0.95 points vs B1 [−1.55, −0.34], from more stuck episodes.
5. **Gazebo ghost tracks**: in the Gazebo smoke test the shared tracker confirmed static objects as people for > 2 s in 10/10 (C1) and 9/10
   (B1) static episodes; the cause is CHAMP body wobble (|roll| p95 8.1° vs ≈0.6° on the real robot) making the 16-beam LiDAR cut objects at
   different heights, not a timing error. The Gazebo TEST (C1 80.7 % vs B1 65.7 %) carries this artefact.
6. **Simulated pedestrians turn ≈1.7× more** than real ones in the THÖR dataset [@rudenko2020thor] (> 60° turns within 2 s ≈2.8×); since the gain
   concentrates on late-turning pedestrians, the real-world benefit may be smaller.
7. **CHAMP fidelity is unresolved**: `champ2` predicts motion better but ranks planners less like Gazebo (ρ 0.59 vs 0.90; difference
   inconclusive).
8. **Re-implemented baselines with deviations** (two-disc body, rescaled/tuned weights, analytic probability instead of Monte Carlo, no
   multi-modal turning model for DRA\*; tracked rather than ground-truth pedestrians for PGIF\*).
9. **Inverse-crime risk**: RespModel's tanh form matches the `champ` regime; tracker centroid compensation matches fastsim's circular
   pedestrians; Isaac shares rays, crowd and tracker with fastsim.
10. **Real-robot dynamics partly assumed**: gain, delay and time constants of the real Go2 are literature values or assumptions.
11. Other: the any-contact metric is sensitive to shallow contacts; pedestrian density and the distracted fraction are assumptions; the
    Helbing-type model has not yet been checked line by line against [@helbing1995social]; confirmation seeds lie in the nominal development
    range; occlusion is not modelled explicitly; timing on the onboard computer is unmeasured.

## 9. Conclusion
For a quadruped that is about three times slower than surrounding pedestrians, a risk-aware MPPI with a discounted max-over-time
geometric risk, evasive primitive samples and a corrected temperature improved collision-free navigation over tuned MPPI, a PGIF-MPPI
re-implementation and a DRA-MPPI re-implementation in a pre-registered, hash-frozen simulation test of 35 000 episodes, with replication on
four further seed sets. The improvement is condition-dependent and small against DRA-MPPI, and the method slightly degrades static-only
navigation. The advantage over tuned MPPI also held in a separate pre-registered Gazebo test with the CHAMP gait (80.7 % vs 65.7 %,
p = 1.5e-13) and with an independent learned-policy body in Isaac Lab (88.5 % vs 79.5 %). On that body C1 was indistinguishable from DRA-MPPI
(88.5 % vs 87.2 %, p = 0.46). Real-robot experiments are the next step ⟦pending⟧. The evaluation package (frozen code hashes, raw logs and a single
deterministic analysis script) is provided to support independent verification.
